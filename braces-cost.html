What this calculator estimates

Estimate the cost of a full course of braces, including the quoted treatment price, orthodontist and facility fees, imaging, retainers and any insurance coverage you enter.

How to use it

Enter the number of treatment cases; one full course of braces for one person is one case. Cost per unit or treatment is the quoted price for one case. Provider or specialist fee takes any orthodontist charge listed separately, and facility, imaging or laboratory fees take imaging or records charges on their own lines. Other related costs collects any remaining quoted items that do not fit the fields above. Enter the coverage percentage from your plan documents (the default of 35% is an example input, not a statement about any plan), the remaining deductible, and any copay not already folded into the percentage, then select Calculate to update the results.

How the calculation works

The bill adds one case at $5,800 plus the fee fields: $5,800 + $500 + $250 + $450 = $7,000. The $500 deductible comes off first, leaving $6,500, and 35% of that is $2,275. The $100 copay is added to the patient side, so the patient cost is $500 + $4,225 + $100 = $4,825, and the insurer contribution shows the remainder, $7,000 - $4,825 = $2,175. Because the copay sits on top of the percentage split, the insurer line is $100 lower than 35% of the post-deductible amount. This copay handling is the calculator's simplified arithmetic, not an insurance plan rule.

How to interpret the result

Read the main figure as the out-of-pocket cost under your entered assumptions. The deductible and copay row, $600 here, is the fixed part of your share that does not depend on the coverage percentage. The insurer contribution is an arithmetic remainder, not a commitment from any plan. Because braces are priced per case, the per-unit line repeats the main figure and becomes useful mainly when comparing a quote that covers more than one case.

Important limitations

Use insurer-allowed charges for covered services. The model does not apply an annual benefit cap, out-of-pocket maximum, prior spending, exclusions or balance billing. Every amount you enter here, including other related costs, is treated as covered by the percentage, so keep non-covered charges out of all fields and add them to the result yourself afterward. Confirm copay/coinsurance treatment with the plan to avoid counting one cost twice. The patient and insurer split is not an adjudication of a claim; actual plans apply their own deductible, coinsurance and copay rules.

Common mistakes

Splitting a quote incorrectly is the main risk: enter each quoted line in exactly one field, so the appliance price goes to cost per unit and a separate orthodontist charge goes to provider fee, never both. Working out 35% of the full $7,000 bill in your head overstates the insurer share, because the percentage applies only after the deductible. Entering the plan's full annual deductible when part is already met overstates your share. Treating the insurer line as a guaranteed payment ignores that it is only a remainder from your inputs.

Reading the who-pays-what breakdown for a treatment plan

Here the 35% in the coverage field is the plan share of the bill after the deductible, so your share is the other 65%. On $7,000.00 of charges that gives $500.00 of deductible, $4,225.00 as your percentage share and a $100.00 copay: you pay $4,825.00 and the estimated plan contribution is $2,175.00. Most of the result sits in the percentage line, 60.4% of the charges.

The what-if table shows what each assumption is worth. Ten more points of plan share lowers your cost by $650.00, a met deductible by $175.00 and no copay by $100.00. If your plan states a dollar maximum for this benefit, compare it with the estimated plan contribution above; the page does not apply one, so it can understate your cost when the maximum is lower than what the percentage would pay.

Frequently asked questions

Does the estimate show what my plan will actually pay?

No. It applies the percentage and deductible you enter; it does not check eligibility, waiting periods or annual maximums. Confirm benefits with the plan before relying on the split.

Why is the insurer contribution $2,175 and not 35% of the bill?

The percentage applies only after the $500 deductible, and the extra $100 copay moves that amount to the patient side: 35% of $6,500 is $2,275, less $100 is $2,175.

Is the $5,800 starting price a typical cost for braces?

No figure on this page is a market rate. The starting values are example inputs; use the numbers from your own written quote.
