What this calculator estimates

Compare an adjustable-rate mortgage payment before and after the first rate reset.

Inputs and units

Fill in each field, then select Calculate to update the results. Required fields cannot be blank.

  • Loan amount ($): the amount borrowed. It must be greater than zero.
  • Initial interest rate (%): the annual rate during the fixed period.
  • Initial fixed period (years): how long the initial rate holds; the page requires the total term to be longer than this.
  • Rate after reset (%): the assumed annual rate after the fixed period ends. It is an assumption you set, not a forecast; actual adjustments follow the loan's index, margin and caps.
  • Total loan term: the full amortization period from the dropdown: 15, 20 or 30 years.

Method and assumptions

The calculator amortizes the loan over the full term at the initial rate for the starting payment, computes the remaining balance after the fixed-period payments, then re-amortizes that balance over the remaining years at the assumed reset rate. The headline is the post-reset payment, with the initial payment, the balance at reset and the change beside it.

With the illustrative starting inputs: the initial payment on 400,000 over 30 years at 5.5% is $2,271.16. After 60 payments the balance at reset is $369,842.41. Re-amortizing that balance over the remaining 25 years at 7% gives $2,613.97, an increase of $342.81 per month.

Reading the result

  • The post-reset payment is the headline: $2,613.97 on the starting inputs, the figure to test your budget against before the fixed period ends.
  • Balance at reset ($369,842.41) shows how little principal the early payments retire on these inputs; most of the 30-year term still lies ahead when the rate resets.
  • The reset rate is your assumption, not a prediction; run low and high values to bracket the payment, since actual adjustments follow the note's index, margin and caps, as the limitations note says.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the reset rate as the rate the loan will actually adjust to; it is a scenario input, and the loan documents govern the real adjustment.
  • Comparing only the initial payments when shopping an ARM against a fixed loan; the post-reset payment is the risk being priced.
  • Entering the fixed period as months; the field takes years.
  • Setting the total term equal to the fixed period; the page requires a longer term so a reset period exists.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the balance at reset so close to the original loan?

Early amortization payments are mostly interest. On the starting inputs, 60 payments of $2,271.16 reduce a 400,000 balance only to $369,842.41, which is typical of a long term at a mid-single-digit rate.

Can the payment fall after reset?

Yes, if you enter a reset rate below the initial rate. Nothing in the model forces an increase; the payment follows the rate assumption you set.

Does this model rate caps or indexes?

No. It applies one assumed reset rate for the whole remaining term. Actual ARMs adjust periodically within cap structures described in the loan documents; the limitations note directs you there for real terms.

Important limitations

This scenario models one reset, followed by an unchanged rate. It does not select an index or margin, apply periodic or lifetime rate caps, or include taxes, insurance, fees or later resets. The initial fixed period must be shorter than the total term. A lender's contractual schedule can differ.
