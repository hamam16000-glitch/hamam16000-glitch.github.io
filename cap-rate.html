Quick summary: Calculate capitalization rate from property value and net operating income.

Formula, worked scenarios and limitations

Keep the operating property separate from the loan

This form subtracts annual operating expenses from annual gross rent, then divides that operating income by the entered property value. Enter all money amounts in one currency and keep rent and expenses on the same annual basis.

The expense field should exclude mortgage principal and interest for this calculation. Include the vacancy allowance, maintenance, management or other operating costs that belong in your analysis explicitly; the form does not estimate them for you.

Reproduce the starting property

Net operating income = annual rent − annual operating expenses. Cap rate = net operating income ÷ positive property value × 100.

At a value of 600,000, rent of 72,000 and expenses of 24,000, operating income is 48,000 and cap rate is 8.00%. The monthly operating income equivalent is 4,000 before financing and any other excluded items.

The same property can show a different rate at a different price

Keep operating income at 48,000 and change property value from 600,000 to 640,000. The rate becomes 7.50%; no rent or operating expense changed. Conversely, a rate can rise because the value falls, which should not be mistaken for an improvement in operations.

For an operating stress, keep value at 600,000 and reduce rent to 64,800 while leaving expenses at 24,000. Operating income becomes 40,800 and the modeled rate is 6.80%.

What the percentage leaves out

The result is not total investment return, a valuation opinion or a forecast of sale proceeds. It excludes appreciation, financing, purchase and sale transaction costs and the timing of capital work unless you have deliberately included a relevant operating allowance.

A loss scenario can have negative operating income and a negative cap rate. A zero property value makes the division undefined. Use DSCR to compare the operating income with entered debt service, and cash-on-cash return when the denominator is invested cash.

Reading the result

  • The headline is the capitalization rate: 8.00% on the starting inputs, the year's net operating income as a share of the 600,000 property value.
  • The net operating income ($48,000.00 a year, $4,000.00 a month) is gross rent minus the entered operating expenses, before any loan payments.
  • Because the loan stays out of the model, the rate compares properties on operations alone; financing changes the cash an investor keeps, not this percentage.

Common mistakes

  • Subtracting loan payments in the expense field; the model prices operations only, and including debt service understates the rate.
  • Entering monthly rent in the annual field; both the rent and expense fields are per year, so a monthly figure understates income twelvefold.
  • Reading the rate as a return guarantee; the model divides the entered income by the entered value, while actual rent, vacancy and expenses move, so verify the inputs against the property's own records.

Frequently asked questions

Does the cap rate include the mortgage?

No. The model subtracts operating expenses only, so the 8.00% on the starting inputs reflects the property's operations before any financing.

What raises the rate?

Higher net operating income or a lower value. On the starting inputs, cutting expenses by $12,000.00 would raise the income to $60,000.00 and the rate to 10.00%.

Is a higher cap rate always better?

The model only computes the ratio from your figures. A higher rate can reflect a lower price or weaker income expectations, so read it with the property's own records and, for a purchase decision, a licensed professional.
