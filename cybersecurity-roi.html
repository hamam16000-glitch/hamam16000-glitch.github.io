What this calculator estimates

Estimate expected financial ROI from a cybersecurity investment.

How to use it

Annual probability of a significant incident is your estimate of the chance of one incident in a year, entered as a percentage. Estimated financial loss per incident is the total cost you expect if one occurs, including response, downtime and recovery. Expected risk reduction from investment is the share of that expected loss you believe the spending removes; the starting 60% is an example input, not a measured guarantee. Annual cybersecurity program cost is the recurring spend, and one-time implementation cost is the upfront spend. Select Calculate to update the results.

How the calculation works

Expected annual loss multiplies probability by impact: 20% x $500,000 = $100,000. Expected loss avoided applies the risk reduction: $100,000 x 60% = $60,000. First-year cost adds the program and implementation: $75,000 + $25,000 = $100,000. Expected net benefit is $60,000 - $100,000 = -$40,000, so ROI is -$40,000 / $100,000 = -40% for the starting scenario.

How to interpret the result

A negative result means the modeled expected savings do not cover the first-year cost under your assumptions; the starting scenario is exactly that case. Because implementation is one-time, later years carry only the annual cost, and in this example $60,000 of expected avoided loss against a $75,000 annual cost stays negative every year. The output moves sharply with the probability, loss and reduction you assume, so use it to test how those assumptions drive the answer rather than as a verdict.

Important limitations

Cyber risk is uncertain and correlated. Regulatory, reputational, operational and catastrophic losses may exceed entered values.

ROI is undefined when the total modeled cost is zero. The associated cash-flow amounts can still be calculated; an undefined return percentage is not a zero-percent return.

Common mistakes

Entering the probability as a fraction (0.2) instead of a percent (20) shrinks the expected loss a hundred-fold; the field takes a percentage. Treating the risk-reduction percentage as measured rather than assumed is the largest error source, because the avoided loss scales directly with it. Counting only the one-time implementation and forgetting the annual program cost overstates later-year returns. Entering the implementation cost again when modeling a later year double-counts it.

Frequently asked questions

Does a negative ROI mean the investment is pointless?

Not necessarily. The model prices only expected financial loss; it does not value contractual, regulatory or reputational requirements, and the result is sensitive to the probability and reduction you assume.

Why is the starting scenario negative?

With the example inputs the expected avoided loss ($60,000) is below the first-year cost ($100,000), so the net is -$40,000. The scenario is illustrative; your own estimates decide the sign.

What does the expected loss figure represent?

A probability-weighted average: a 20% annual chance of a $500,000 incident counts as $100,000 per year when averaged over many years. It is not a prediction that you will lose $100,000 in any single year.
