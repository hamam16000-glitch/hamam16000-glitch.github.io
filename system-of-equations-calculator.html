What this calculator estimates

Solve two linear equations with two unknowns using the determinant method.

Inputs and units

Fill in each field, then select Calculate to update the results. Required fields cannot be blank.

  • Equation 1: x coefficient, y coefficient, result: the three numbers of the first equation in the form a x plus b y equals c.
  • Equation 2: x coefficient, y coefficient, result: the three numbers of the second equation in the same form.

Method and assumptions

The calculator scales each equation by its largest coefficient for numerical stability, then solves the two-by-two system by the determinant method: it computes the determinant, and when it is nonzero, divides the modified determinants to get x and y. A zero determinant means no unique solution, and the classification line says which case occurred.

With the starting inputs 2x + y = 7 and x - y = 2: the determinant is 2 times -1 minus 1 times 1, which is -3, so there is one unique solution. The result x = 3, y = 1 checks out: 2 times 3 plus 1 is 7, and 3 minus 1 is 2.

Worked example

The equations 0x + 0y = 1 and 0x + 0y = 2 are inconsistent. The equations 0.00000001x = 0.00000001 and 0.00000001y = 0.00000001 have the unique solution x=1, y=1.

Reading the result

  • The headline gives x and y together; the separate x and y lines repeat each value on its own row.
  • Determinant tells you how close the system sits to having no unique solution; values near zero mean the two equations are nearly parallel, which the limitations note flags as sensitive to input rounding.
  • Solution classification reads One unique solution here; the preserved worked example shows the inconsistent case 0x + 0y = 1 versus 0x + 0y = 2, which can never hold at once.

Common mistakes

  • Entering the constant on the wrong field; the form is a x plus b y equals c, with the result in the third field.
  • Forgetting a sign; the second starting equation needs y coefficient -1, not 1.
  • Expecting exact answers for nearly parallel equations; the limitations note says near-singular systems are sensitive to input rounding.
  • Treating the page as a symbolic solver; the limitations note says it is numeric, not a symbolic algebra system.

Frequently asked questions

What does a zero determinant mean?

The equations are parallel: either no solution exists or infinitely many do. The classification line distinguishes the cases, and the preserved example shows both an inconsistent pair and a tiny-coefficient pair that still solves uniquely.

Why does the calculator scale the equations first?

Dividing each row by its largest coefficient keeps the determinant arithmetic numerically stable, so very large or very small coefficients behave better; the displayed determinant is computed from your original coefficients.

Can it solve three equations?

No; this page handles two equations in two unknowns only.

Limitations

Calculations use floating-point numbers. Near-singular systems can be sensitive to input rounding; this is not a symbolic algebra system. A tiny nonzero determinant is displayed in scientific notation when necessary.
