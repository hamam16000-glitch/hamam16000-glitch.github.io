Simple Interest, Compound Growth or a Loan Payment?

Compare three interest models, using explicit balance, timing and payment assumptions.

Updated November 4, 2026

The question determines the model

“How much interest?” can refer to an unchanged principal, a balance that grows, or a loan whose principal is repaid over time. A calculator needs to know which balance receives interest and when money is added or removed. Selecting a plausible rate without selecting the right model can produce a precise answer to the wrong question.

Model one: an unchanged principal

Simple interest applies the rate to the original principal for the whole period. A principal of 5,000 at 6% per year for 18 months produces 5,000 × 0.06 × 1.5 = 450 of interest. The total is 5,450. This is useful for the explicitly stated constant-principal relationship; it does not reconstruct a loan with monthly principal repayments.

Simple interest = principal × annual decimal rate × time in years

Model two: interest stays in the balance

With compounding, a month’s interest remains in the account and changes the balance that receives the next month’s interest. Contributions add another timing choice: money added at the beginning of a month receives that month’s growth, while an end-of-month contribution does not.

Separate contributions from assumed growth

Start with 1,000, add 100 at the end of each month, use a nominal annual rate of 6% divided into twelve 0.5% monthly periods, and continue for one year. The modeled balance is approximately 2,295.23. Of that, 2,200 is money paid in and 95.23 is modeled growth. At 0%, the balance would simply be 2,200.

A nominal rate divided by twelve is different from an already effective annual yield. Compounding a nominal 6% monthly gives about 6.1678% effective growth over a full year before other effects. A product’s stated yield, fees and crediting convention need to match the model before comparing outputs.

Model three: a fixed payment repays the loan

A fully amortizing fixed-rate loan uses the outstanding balance each month. Part of each payment covers interest and the rest reduces principal. For 300,000 at 6.5% over 30 years, the modeled monthly principal-and-interest payment is about 1,896.20. First-month interest is 1,625, leaving about 271.20 for principal. The next month starts with a slightly smaller balance.

Multiplying the original 300,000 by the annual rate and the full 30 years would ignore those repayments. Conversely, a savings-growth formula would ignore the required payment that reduces the loan. The models are not interchangeable.

Compare assumptions before comparing results

  • Is the rate nominal or already an effective annual figure?
  • Does the balance stay unchanged, grow, or fall as principal is repaid?
  • Are additions at the start or end of a period?
  • Are fees, tax, inflation and product-specific rules included?
  • Does the result show interest separately from money paid in or principal repaid?

These tools compute scenarios rather than predict returns or supply lender terms. The simple-interest calculator, compound-interest calculator and loan-payment calculator each state their assumptions and provide examples.
