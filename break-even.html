    Quick summary: Estimate whole units needed to cover fixed costs, include a percentage sales fee, and check profit at a planned sales volume.

    Updated November 24, 2026
    Read the complete guide

    Updated reference sections in this guide are in English.

    Use one consistent sales period

    Enter the fixed costs for a period such as a month and the planned unit sales for that same period. Selling price and variable cost are per unit. An optional percentage fee is applied to gross sales revenue, which reduces the amount each sale contributes toward fixed costs. Enter 0 when no such fee is included.

    Contribution pays for fixed costs

    Contribution per unit = price × (1 − fee percentage ÷ 100) − variable cost
    Fractional break-even units = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit
    Whole units to cover costs = ceiling(fractional units)
    Profit at planned volume = units × contribution per unit − fixed costs

    The break-even division requires a positive contribution when fixed costs are positive. If every additional sale contributes zero or less, selling more cannot cover those fixed costs in this constant-price model. Rounding up gives a whole-unit threshold; a fraction of a unit cannot be sold when units are indivisible.

    Check the threshold and a sales plan

    3,000 fixed costs, a 50 price and a 20 variable cost

    With no sales fee, each unit contributes 30. Covering 3,000 requires 100 units, with 5,000 of revenue at that threshold. At 150 units, revenue is 7,500, total modeled costs are 6,000, and profit is 1,500.

    Add a 10% fee on revenue

    The fee takes 5 from each 50 sale, so contribution falls to 25. The threshold becomes 120 units. At 150 planned units, profit is 750 under the same fixed and variable cost assumptions.

    Keep fixed, variable and percentage costs distinct

    Do not count the same fee both inside variable cost and again as a percentage of revenue. This model covers one consistent unit or a genuinely comparable unit mix. Volume discounts, capacity steps, unsold inventory, taxes, different product margins and changing prices are not modeled. With zero fixed costs, zero sales covers those fixed costs, but a nonpositive contribution still does not produce a positive contribution at greater volume.

    Reference and questions

    U.S. Small Business Administration: break-even calculator gives the basic fixed-cost and contribution relationship. This version also exposes a sales-fee assumption and a planned-volume profit calculation.

    Why round a fractional unit upward?

    The threshold represents enough whole units to cover costs. If the theoretical threshold is 100.2 units, 101 whole units are required.

    Why is there no positive-sales break-even point?

    With positive fixed costs and contribution of zero or less per unit, increasing sales does not recover the fixed cost in this model.

    Does the tool forecast how many units I will sell?

    No. Planned unit sales and all cost assumptions are entered by you.

    Reading the result

    • The headline is the whole-unit break-even point: 100 units on the starting inputs, the smallest whole unit count whose contribution covers the 3,000 of fixed costs.
    • The contribution per unit ($30.00) is the selling price after the entered sales fee minus the variable cost; each unit sold adds that much toward fixed costs first and profit after.
    • At the planned 150 units the model shows revenue of $7,500.00 against total modeled costs of $6,000.00, a profit of $1,500.00, so the plan sits 50 units above the threshold.

    Common mistakes

    • Mixing time periods; the fixed costs, the planned units and the revenue lines all describe the same period, so a monthly fixed-cost figure needs a monthly unit plan.
    • Entering the total variable cost instead of the per-unit cost; the model multiplies the variable figure by the planned units, so a total overstates costs.
    • Leaving the sales fee at zero when the sales channel charges one; the fee lowers the per-unit contribution, so a missing fee understates the break-even point.
    • Reading the threshold as a sales forecast; the model only computes the arithmetic of the entered figures, and actual sales depend on demand the model cannot see.

    Frequently asked questions

    Why does the calculator round the break-even point up?

    Units sell whole. The exact threshold can be fractional, so the headline shows the smallest whole unit count that covers the fixed costs, 100 units on the starting inputs.

    What happens as the price barely covers the variable cost?

    The contribution per unit shrinks and the threshold climbs. On the starting inputs the contribution is $30.00 per unit; halving it would double the units needed to 200.

    Does the planned-profit line include the fixed costs?

    Yes. It subtracts them once: 150 times $30.00 minus $3,000.00, which is $1,500.00 on the starting inputs.
